What to look for

The magnets trade energy with the moving parts. They don't add net energy on every turn.

01

The kick is the starting energy

The kick spins the flywheel. In this model, doubling the starting RPM gives four times the initial kinetic energy, because energy rises with speed squared.

02

Magnets help, then resist

As the magnets move apart, magnetic potential energy can turn into motion. Bringing them back to the same arrangement takes that energy back. Net magnetic work over a full cycle is zero.

03

Useful power slows the shaft

Take power out to move a car or run a generator and the flywheel loses energy. More load can mean more power at first, but also a faster drop in speed. With zero load, output power is zero.

Model assumptions and equations

This is a one-degree-of-freedom teaching model, not a magnetic-field solver or a calibrated replica of the video. It represents the magnet/crank interaction with a smooth, periodic potential. The actual force curve for specific neodymium magnets would require their dimensions, grade, orientation, gap, and a measured setup.

Um(θ) = Em(1 − cos θ)   ·   τm = −dUm/dθ = −Em sin θ

The rotor obeys I θ̈ = τm − (cload + cdrag) θ̇. The model's simple load behaves like a generator with Pload = cload ω². Since the magnetic potential returns to its starting value after a full turn, the magnets contribute zero net work per complete cycle. Starting at the low-energy position, this model has a barrier of 2Em: a smaller kick rocks back and forth rather than making full turns. With enough kick energy and zero drag and load, the rotor can coast indefinitely—but delivers no power.

For a real engine's run time or car performance, you'd need measured RPM, shaft torque, load, and a proper energy balance. This demo uses an illustrative flywheel inertia of 0.08 kg·m², so its wattage is not a prediction for a full-size car.
